When connecting a portable generator to your home, you have two main options: a generator interlock kit or a transfer switch. Both prevent dangerous backfeeding, but they work differently and have distinct advantages.

This guide compares both options in detail to help you choose the right solution for your situation.

## What's the Difference?

### Generator Interlock

A generator interlock is a metal plate that attaches to your electrical panel cover. It creates a mechanical barrier that prevents your main breaker and generator breaker from being on at the same time.

**How it works:**
1. Turn OFF the main breaker
2. The interlock slides, allowing the generator breaker to turn ON
3. Start your generator
4. Turn on individual breakers as needed (don't exceed generator capacity)

### Transfer Switch

A transfer switch is a separate electrical device (usually next to your panel) with its own breakers for specific circuits. It switches those circuits between utility and generator power.

**How it works:**
1. Start your generator
2. Connect it to the power inlet box
3. Flip the transfer switch from "LINE" to "GEN"
4. Pre-selected circuits now run on generator power

### How an Interlock Works

The interlock kit is a simple mechanical device:

1. **Physical barrier** - A metal plate that slides or pivots
2. **Prevents simultaneous operation** - Main and generator breakers can't both be ON
3. **Break-before-make** - You must turn one OFF before the other can turn ON

This prevents backfeeding because your home is completely disconnected from the utility before generator power is applied.

### Interlock Requirements

For a proper interlock installation, you need:

1. **Compatible panel** - Check manufacturer listings
2. **Open breaker space** - Adjacent to main breaker
3. **Generator breaker** - Sized for your generator (usually 30A or 50A)
4. **Power inlet box** - Outdoor receptacle for generator
5. **Generator cord** - Connects generator to inlet box

### Using an Interlock Safely

**Before an outage (preparation):**
1. Know your generator's wattage capacity
2. List which circuits you'll need to power
3. Calculate the total load of those circuits
4. Practice the switching procedure

**During an outage:**
1. Turn OFF the main breaker
2. Turn OFF all individual circuit breakers
3. Slide the interlock to the generator position
4. Start your generator outdoors
5. Let it warm up for 1-2 minutes
6. Turn ON the generator breaker
7. Turn ON individual circuits ONE AT A TIME
8. Monitor generator—don't exceed capacity

**When utility power returns:**
1. Turn OFF all individual circuit breakers
2. Turn OFF the generator breaker
3. Slide interlock to utility position
4. Turn ON the main breaker
5. Turn ON individual circuits
6. Shut down generator

<Callout type="warning" title="Load Management Is Critical">
With an interlock, YOU are responsible for not overloading the generator. Adding too many loads can damage the generator, trip breakers, or cause brown-out damage to appliances. Know your loads!
</Callout>

### How a Transfer Switch Works

A transfer switch is a completely separate switching device:

1. **Pre-wired circuits** - 6-12 circuits connected during installation
2. **Two-position switches** - Each circuit flips between LINE (utility) and GEN (generator)
3. **Mechanical interlock** - Circuits can't connect to both sources simultaneously

### Transfer Switch Circuit Selection

During installation, you choose which circuits connect to the transfer switch. Common choices:

**Essential circuits (usually include):**
- Refrigerator
- Freezer
- Sump pump
- Well pump
- Furnace/HVAC
- Security system

**Nice-to-have circuits:**
- Kitchen outlets
- Lighting (one or two rooms)
- Garage door opener
- Internet/router
- TV/entertainment

<Callout type="tip" title="Plan Carefully">
Changing circuits later requires an electrician and rewiring. Think carefully about what you'll need during an extended outage when making your selections.
</Callout>

## Safety Comparison

Both options are safe when properly installed and used correctly. The key differences are:

### Interlock Safety Considerations

**Requires user discipline:**
- Must turn off main breaker first
- Must manage loads manually
- Must follow proper sequence

**Failure modes:**
- User error (forgetting steps)
- Overloading generator
- Starting loads too quickly

### Transfer Switch Safety Considerations

**More foolproof operation:**
- Physical switches prevent errors
- Pre-selected circuits can't overload
- Simpler operation

**Failure modes:**
- Mechanical switch failure (rare)
- Improper initial circuit selection

### Code Compliance

Both are NEC-compliant:

| Requirement | Interlock | Transfer Switch |
|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Backfeed prevention | Yes | Yes |
| UL listing available | Yes | Yes |
| Permits required | Yes | Yes |
| Professional install recommended | Yes | Usually required |

<Callout type="info">
Some jurisdictions have specific preferences. Check with your local building department before deciding. Some areas require transfer switches; others accept either option.
</Callout>

## Which Should You Choose?

### Choose a Generator Interlock If:

- **Budget is primary concern** - Save $300-$500
- **You're electrically knowledgeable** - Comfortable managing loads
- **You want flexibility** - Different circuits each outage
- **Quick installation needed** - Can be done in 1-2 hours
- **You might upgrade generators** - Easy to change later
- **You have limited essential circuits** - Don't need more than 10-12

### Choose a Transfer Switch If:

- **Simplicity matters most** - Just flip switches
- **Multiple family members will use it** - Easy operation
- **You're less electrically confident** - Pre-selected circuits are safer
- **You want consistent essentials** - Same circuits every time
- **You may add automation later** - Some switches can upgrade
- **Your local code prefers it** - Check with building department

## Hybrid Options

Some products combine features of both:

### Load Center Interlocks

These install like interlocks but include a small subpanel for critical circuits. You get interlock flexibility plus dedicated essential circuit protection.

### Upgradeable Transfer Switches

Products like the Generac Home Link can start as manual transfer switches and later upgrade to automatic transfer with a standby generator, giving you a future growth path.

## Installation Considerations

### Panel Compatibility

**Interlocks:** Must match your specific panel brand and model. Common compatibility:
- Square D Homeline/QO
- GE
- Siemens/Murray
- Cutler-Hammer/Eaton
- Challenger

**Transfer switches:** Compatible with most any panel since they connect to existing breakers.

### Space Requirements

**Interlocks:** Need one open breaker space adjacent to main breaker.

**Transfer switches:** Need wall space next to main panel (usually 18" x 12" minimum).

### Inlet Box Location

Both require an outdoor inlet box:
- Within 25-50 feet of generator location
- Protected from direct rain/snow
- Accessible but not easily damaged
- Away from windows (exhaust fumes)
